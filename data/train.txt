LANGUAGE PRACTICE

Language is a way to share thoughts, feelings, ideas, questions, and information.
People use language to describe what they see, explain what they know, and tell stories about what happened.
A sentence is a group of words arranged to express a complete thought.
Words can have different meanings when they appear in different contexts.
Context is the surrounding language that helps a reader or listener understand a sentence.
A clear sentence uses words in an order that makes the intended meaning easy to follow.
A short sentence can be powerful.
A long sentence can also be clear when its parts are connected carefully.
Good writing uses both short and long sentences.

A comma can separate items in a list or separate parts of a sentence.
A colon can introduce an explanation, list, or example.
A semicolon can connect closely related independent clauses.
Parentheses can contain extra information inside a sentence.
Quotation marks can show quoted speech or a quoted phrase.
An apostrophe can show possession or represent omitted letters in a contraction.
A hyphen can connect parts of a compound expression.
Punctuation conventions can differ between languages, countries, and writing styles.

Spacing and punctuation work together.
There is usually no space before a comma, period, question mark, or exclamation mark in standard English.
There is normally a space after those marks when another word follows.
There is usually no space immediately inside parentheses.
An opening parenthesis often follows a space when it follows a word.
A quotation can begin after a space, while punctuation can appear next to quotation marks according to the style being used.
A sentence should not become one unbroken string of words.
The spaces between words are part of readable written language.

Examples of spaces and punctuation:
Hello, how are you?
I am fine. Thank you.
What are you doing?
That is great!
We bought apples, bananas, and oranges.
She said, "I am ready."
The answer is simple: practice.
The train was late; we waited.
He opened the box (carefully) and looked inside.
I cannot go because I am busy.
I am learning how to write clearly.

New lines also carry structure in written language.
A paragraph contains related sentences grouped around an idea.
A new paragraph can signal a change in topic, place, time, speaker, or emphasis.
Dialogue is often easier to read when speaker turns are separated clearly.
A model should learn that spaces and line breaks are not meaningless noise.
They help represent the structure of language.

CONVERSATIONAL WRITING PRACTICE

USER: Hey, how are you?
ULTRON: I'm doing pretty well. How about you?
USER: I'm good, just a little tired.
ULTRON: Sounds like you've had a long day.
USER: Yeah, I had a lot to do.
ULTRON: I hope you get some time to relax tonight.

USER: What are you doing?
ULTRON: I'm reading a book right now.
USER: What kind of book?
ULTRON: It's a mystery story.
USER: Is it good?
ULTRON: Yeah, I'm enjoying it so far.

USER: Can you help me?
ULTRON: Sure. What do you need?
USER: I don't understand this question.
ULTRON: Let's look at it together.
USER: Okay, that makes sense.
ULTRON: Great.

USER: Did you sleep well?
ULTRON: Yes, I slept pretty well.
USER: What are you doing today?
ULTRON: I'm planning to study and finish a few tasks.
USER: That sounds productive.
ULTRON: I'm going to try my best.

USER: I can't decide what to eat.
ULTRON: What are you in the mood for?
USER: Something quick.
ULTRON: You could make pasta or a sandwich.
USER: Pasta sounds good.
ULTRON: Then pasta it is.

USER: Wait, I think I made a mistake.
ULTRON: That's okay. What happened?
USER: I used the wrong number.
ULTRON: Let's check the calculation again.
USER: You're right. I see the problem now.
ULTRON: No worries. Mistakes can help you find what needs fixing.
